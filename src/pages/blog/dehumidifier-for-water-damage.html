Short answer: Yes, a dehumidifier helps with water damage by pulling the moisture that wet materials release out of the air. For a small, clean leak, run it with fans for several days until moisture readings return to normal. Large or soaked areas need professional LGR or desiccant dehumidifiers, because household units can't keep up.

After the water is gone, the hard part starts: getting the water out of the walls, floors and framing. Drying is mostly about air. Fans move it, and dehumidifiers pull the water out of it. DryFlare uses commercial dehumidifiers and air movers for water mitigation and structural drying, but a home unit can handle small jobs.

Does a dehumidifier help with water damage?

Yes. When wet drywall, wood and carpet dry, their water goes into the air. If nothing removes it, the room stays humid and the materials stop drying. A dehumidifier pulls that water out of the air and into a tank or drain hose. It works best with fans and with the room closed up.

The EPA advises drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold. The CDC gives the same 24-to-48-hour window after floods. That makes the first two days the most important.

How long to run a dehumidifier after water damage

Run it until the materials are dry, not just until the surface feels dry. For a small leak that's often three to five days. Bigger jobs take longer. Things that slow drying:

  • Dense materials: hardwood, plaster and concrete hold water for a long time
  • Trapped spaces: inside walls, under cabinets and under floors
  • Cold rooms: household units slow down below about 65°F
  • High outdoor humidity: every door opening brings in wet air
  • Undersized equipment: one small unit in a big wet basement

The only reliable way to know is a moisture meter. Compare wet areas with a dry wall or floor in the same house. When they match, you're done.

How to use a dehumidifier for water damage

  1. Remove standing water first. A dehumidifier can't drain a flooded floor. Use a pump or wet-dry vacuum.
  2. Pull out what can't be dried. Wet carpet pad, soaked insulation and swollen particleboard usually go. The CDC says to discard drywall and insulation soaked by floodwater or sewage.
  3. Close the room. Shut windows and doors so the unit isn't drying the outdoors.
  4. Set up fans. Point them across wet surfaces, not straight at one spot.
  5. Run the dehumidifier on continuous. Use a drain hose if you can, so it doesn't stop when the tank fills.
  6. Check readings daily. Keep going until wet areas read the same as dry ones.

Household vs. professional dehumidifiers

Restoration crews use bigger, stronger machines than you'd buy for a basement. The difference shows in how fast a room dries.

TypeBest forLimits
Household (refrigerant)Small leaks, one room, routine basement humiditySlows in cool rooms; small tank; low capacity
LGR (low-grain refrigerant)Most professional water damage jobsHeavy and loud; rented or used by crews
DesiccantCold spaces, hardwood floors, dense materialsNeeds ducting; uses more power

LGR stands for low-grain refrigerant. It means the unit can dry air to a much lower moisture level than a home model, so it keeps pulling water when a household unit would stall.

What humidity should you aim for?

The EPA recommends keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, and ideally between 30 and 50 percent. During active drying, lower is better because dry air pulls water out of materials faster. A cheap hygrometer shows the reading. Once drying is done, keep a basement under 50 percent to stop musty smells and mold.

Signs a dehumidifier isn't enough

  • The tank fills every few hours for days and readings don't drop
  • Walls stay cool and damp to the touch
  • Paint is bubbling or baseboards are swelling
  • A musty smell is getting stronger
  • Water got inside walls, under hardwood or into a crawl space

These are the cases where crews open wall cavities, use injection drying for walls and floors, or tent hardwood. The full water damage restoration process shows where drying fits.

How to prevent moisture problems after drying

  • Keep a dehumidifier running in basements during humid months.
  • Run bathroom fans during and after showers.
  • Fix leaks quickly; the EPA calls moisture control the key to mold control.
  • Keep gutters and downspouts working so water stays away from the foundation.
  • Check your sump pump each spring; our sump pump failure guide explains how.

Local note: Humid summers make drying slower in Florida, Louisiana and Georgia, so more dehumidifier capacity is needed. In damp Oregon, crawl spaces stay wet for months without one.

When should you call a drying professional?

  • More than one room, or a finished basement, got wet
  • Water sat for more than 24 hours
  • Readings don't drop after two or three days
  • Hardwood, plaster or a crawl space is wet
  • The water was dirty, like sewage or floodwater
