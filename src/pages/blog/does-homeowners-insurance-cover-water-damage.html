Short answer: Yes, homeowners insurance covers water damage when it is sudden and accidental, like a burst pipe or an overflowing washer. It usually does not cover flood from rising water, slow leaks that went on for weeks, or sewer backups unless you added that coverage. Flood needs a separate policy.

When water damages your home, the first question is often "Will insurance pay for this?" The answer depends on where the water came from and how fast it happened. This is general information, not insurance or legal advice. Every policy is different, so read yours and talk with your agent.

What type of water damage is covered by homeowners insurance?

Standard policies cover water damage that is sudden, accidental and comes from inside your home. The NAIC says homeowners policies cover damage from a "sudden and accidental" discharge from a plumbing system.

Usually coveredUsually not covered
A pipe that suddenly burstsFlood from rising water outside
A washing machine hose that splitsA slow leak that went on for weeks
A water heater that suddenly failsDamage from poor maintenance or wear
Rain through a roof damaged by wind or hailSewer or drain backup (without an add-on)
An overflowing toilet or tubGroundwater seeping through the foundation
Water used to put out a fireMold from a long-term moisture problem

Most homeowners carry an HO-3 policy. It covers the house for all risks that aren't excluded, which is why the exclusions matter so much.

Does homeowners insurance cover water damage from a burst pipe?

Yes, in most cases. A burst pipe is the classic sudden and accidental event. But the NAIC notes that policies generally require you to keep the home at a minimum temperature, or shut off the water if it is vacant. A frozen pipe in a house left unheated all winter may be denied. See what to do when a pipe bursts for first steps.

Does homeowners insurance cover water damage from rain?

It depends on how the rain got in. If wind, hail or a falling tree damaged the roof or a window and rain came through the opening, that is usually covered. If rain seeped through an old roof or worn caulking, insurers often treat it as maintenance. Rain that floods your yard and flows inside counts as flood, which needs flood insurance.

Why flood damage needs a separate policy

Standard homeowners policies exclude flood. FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) and private insurers sell flood policies. FloodSmart says NFIP building coverage goes up to $250,000 and contents coverage up to $100,000. Most NFIP policies have a 30-day waiting period, so you can't buy one when a storm is coming and expect it to pay.

Sewer backup, sump pumps and other add-ons

The NAIC lists water backup of sewer as an optional coverage that pays for damage from sewer or drain backup. Without it, a basement full of sewage or water from a failed sump pump is often not covered. The add-on usually has its own limit. Our guides to sewage backup in the basement and sump pump failure explain why this matters.

  • Water backup coverage: sewer, drain and sump pump overflow
  • Service line coverage: repairs to the water or sewer line on your property
  • Equipment breakdown: some mechanical failures of home systems
  • Flood insurance: a separate policy, not an add-on, in most cases

Slow leaks, wear and tear, and mold

Insurance covers sudden events, not upkeep. A leak under a sink that rotted the cabinet over months is usually excluded. So is damage from a water heater or roof that simply wore out. Mold is often limited or excluded unless it came from a covered water loss. The EPA advises drying wet materials within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold, and quick drying also shows your insurer you acted to limit the damage.

How to file a water damage insurance claim

  1. Stop the water. Shut off the main valve or the fixture supply.
  2. Document everything. Take photos and video of the source, the damage and your belongings before cleanup.
  3. Prevent more damage. Policies expect you to take reasonable steps, like water extraction and drying. Keep every receipt.
  4. Call your insurer or agent. Report the claim promptly and write down the claim number.
  5. Keep damaged items until the adjuster sees them, unless they're a health hazard.
  6. Get written estimates and compare them with the adjuster's scope.

Costs depend on the size of the job, so see our guide to water damage restoration cost for what drives the price. If your claim stalls, your state insurance department can help; each state page names the regulator.

How to lower your risk of a denied claim

  • Replace washer hoses and water heater supply lines on schedule.
  • Keep heat on in winter, or shut off and drain the water if you leave.
  • Fix small leaks right away and keep receipts.
  • Add water backup coverage if you have a basement or a sump pump.
  • Buy flood insurance if you live anywhere floods can reach, not just in high-risk zones.

Local note: Rules vary by state. Florida law sets a one-year deadline to report a new property claim. Coastal homes in Texas often get wind cover from the state windstorm association, and many basements in Illinois and Michigan flood from sewer backups that need the add-on.
