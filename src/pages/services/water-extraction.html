Quick answer: Water extraction removes standing water and pulls soaked-in water out of carpet, pad and floors using submersible pumps and truck-mounted or portable extractors. It is the first step of drying a building. The EPA advises drying water-damaged areas within 24 to 48 hours to prevent mold, so extraction should start the same day.

What water extraction includes

  • Finding and stopping the source of water, or confirming a plumber has
  • Checking for electrical hazards before anyone enters the water
  • Pumping standing water with submersible or gas-powered pumps
  • Extracting water from carpet and pad with weighted or truck-mounted extractors
  • Lifting carpet to check the pad and subfloor underneath
  • Moving furniture and contents out of the wet area
  • Moisture mapping with meters and thermal cameras to find hidden water
  • Setting up air movers and dehumidifiers for the drying phase

The water extraction process, step by step

  1. Safety check. The crew confirms the source is off, the power to wet rooms is safe, and the water category, which decides the protective gear.
  2. Bulk water removal. Submersible pumps handle deep water in basements and crawl spaces. Gas-powered trash pumps are used when power is out or water carries debris.
  3. Carpet and floor extraction. Truck-mounted units give the strongest suction for large areas; portable extractors reach upper floors and tight spaces. Weighted extraction tools press water out of carpet and pad.
  4. Moisture mapping. Pin and pinless moisture meters and thermal imaging cameras show where water has wicked into walls, cabinets and subfloors.
  5. Removal of what can't be saved. Soaked pad, swollen baseboards and drywall that wicked up water are removed so the structure can dry.
  6. Hand-off to drying. Air movers and dehumidifiers are placed and the drying goal is set. Read more on water mitigation and structural drying.

Why fast water removal matters

Water keeps moving after the leak stops. It wicks up drywall, soaks under baseboards and flooring, and seeps into subfloors and framing. Particleboard cabinets swell, hardwood cups and paint starts to bubble. The EPA notes that drying water-damaged materials within 24 to 48 hours is key to preventing mold growth. Every hour of delay means more material to remove and a longer, more expensive dry.

Clean water also changes category over time. A supply-line leak that sits for days in a warm house picks up bacteria from the materials it soaks, and the crew may then need to treat it as gray or black water, with more removal and disinfection.

What affects the cost of water extraction

  • Volume and depth of water. An inch of water across a basement is a very different job from a wet carpet in one room.
  • Water category. Gray and black water require protective gear, disinfection and more material removal.
  • Floor type. Carpet and pad hold far more water than tile, vinyl or sealed concrete.
  • Access. Upper floors, crawl spaces and long hose runs take more time and equipment.
  • What happens next. Extraction is usually billed with the drying and any material removal that follows.

Ask the crew for a written estimate once they have inspected the job.

DIY or call a pro?

You can often handle a small spill of clean water on tile, vinyl or sealed concrete with towels, a mop and fans. Call a water removal professional when:

  • Water has soaked carpet, drywall, insulation or wood flooring
  • More than one room is wet, or water reached a lower floor
  • The water came from a toilet, sewer line or outside flooding
  • Water sat for more than a day, or you smell mustiness
  • The power in the wet area may be on, or you can't find the source

Insurance notes for water removal

Homeowners policies usually cover sudden, accidental water damage, such as a burst pipe, and the extraction that follows. They usually exclude long-term leaks and maintenance problems. FEMA notes that most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage, which needs a separate flood policy. Photograph the water before extraction starts and keep the crew's moisture readings; your adjuster will ask for them. This is general information, so check your own policy.
