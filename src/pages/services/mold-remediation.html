Quick answer: Mold remediation fixes the moisture source, contains the moldy area, filters the air with HEPA equipment and removes or cleans moldy materials, then dries the area so mold does not return. The EPA says the key to mold control is moisture control. For mold covering more than about 10 square feet, the EPA recommends following its professional remediation guidance.

What mold remediation includes

  • Inspection to find the mold and the moisture causing it
  • Fixing or confirming repair of the leak or moisture source
  • Containment with plastic sheeting and negative air pressure
  • HEPA air scrubbers to capture airborne spores
  • Removing moldy porous materials such as drywall, insulation and carpet
  • HEPA vacuuming and cleaning of framing and hard surfaces
  • Drying the area with dehumidifiers
  • Final check that the area is clean, dry and free of visible mold and odor

Why mold shows up after water damage

Mold spores are everywhere, indoors and out. They only need moisture and something to feed on, like the paper face of drywall, wood, carpet backing or dust. The EPA notes that indoor mold growth can and should be controlled by controlling moisture. When a leak, flood or sewage backup leaves materials wet for more than a day or two, mold has what it needs. That is why fast structural drying is the best mold prevention there is.

The mold remediation process

  1. Inspect and plan. Find the full extent of the mold and the moisture causing it. Hidden mold behind walls or under floors is common after leaks.
  2. Fix the moisture. If you clean up mold but don't fix the water problem, the EPA warns, the mold problem will most likely come back.
  3. Contain the area. Plastic sheeting and negative air pressure keep spores from spreading to clean rooms during the work.
  4. Filter the air. HEPA air scrubbers capture spores stirred up during removal.
  5. Remove and clean. Moldy porous materials are bagged and removed; framing and hard surfaces are HEPA-vacuumed and cleaned.
  6. Dry and verify. The area is dried and checked for visible mold and odor before repairs. Some jobs add post-remediation testing by an independent assessor.

DIY mold cleanup or a professional?

The EPA says the size of the problem is one factor in deciding who should clean it. Small areas of surface mold on hard surfaces can often be cleaned by a homeowner wearing gloves, goggles and an N-95 respirator, using detergent and water. Call a professional when:

  • Mold covers more than about 10 square feet, the threshold where the EPA points to its professional guidance
  • The water damage came from sewage or other contaminated water
  • Mold is inside the heating and cooling system or ducts
  • You smell mold but cannot find it
  • Anyone in the home has asthma, allergies, a weakened immune system or other health concerns; talk with a doctor first

Mold testing: when it helps

If mold is visible, testing is usually not the first step: fixing the moisture and removing the mold is. The EPA notes that no federal limits have been set for mold or mold spores, so sampling cannot check a building's compliance with a federal standard. Testing can still be useful to find hidden mold, to answer a health question with a doctor, or to confirm cleanup worked. In several states, including Texas, New York and Florida, mold assessment and mold remediation are licensed activities, and the assessor and the remediator on a job are expected to be separate companies. Check your state's rules before hiring.

Signs of mold in your home

  • A musty, earthy smell, especially in basements, closets and bathrooms
  • Black, green, gray or white spots on drywall, grout, caulk or wood
  • Stains or discoloration after a past leak
  • Peeling paint or warped surfaces on walls with past water damage
  • Allergy-like symptoms that improve when you leave the house

What affects the cost of mold remediation

  • Size of the moldy area and how many rooms it reaches.
  • Location: crawl spaces, attics and wall cavities take more work than open walls.
  • Materials to remove and replace.
  • Containment and air filtration needed.
  • The water problem behind it, which must be fixed first.
  • Testing by an independent assessor, if you choose it or your state requires it.

Insurance notes

Insurance often treats mold as part of a covered water loss when it follows a sudden event like a burst pipe, sometimes with a separate mold limit. Mold from slow leaks, condensation or high humidity is commonly excluded. Reporting water damage quickly and drying it fast helps protect coverage. Read your policy and talk with your agent.
