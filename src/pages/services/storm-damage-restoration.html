Quick answer: Storm damage restoration secures a damaged building with tarps and board-up, removes rainwater that got inside, dries the structure and repairs what the storm and water ruined. Water through a wind- or hail-damaged roof is often covered by homeowners insurance, but FEMA notes that rising floodwater usually is not.

What storm damage restoration includes

  • Safety check for downed lines, gas leaks and structural damage
  • Emergency roof tarping and window and door board-up
  • Removing fallen debris from inside the building
  • Water extraction from floors, carpet and basements
  • Removing wet insulation, drywall and ceiling materials
  • Structural drying with air movers and dehumidifiers
  • Mold prevention and remediation where needed
  • Photos, inventories and moisture logs for your claim

The storm damage restoration process

  1. Safety first. No one enters until the building is safe. Downed lines are left to the utility.
  2. Secure the building. Tarps go over roof openings and plywood over broken windows and doors to stop more water getting in.
  3. Remove water and debris. Extraction equipment removes standing water; debris is cleared from inside. See water extraction.
  4. Tear-out. Wet ceilings, insulation and drywall are removed so the structure can dry.
  5. Dry and monitor. Air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture readings reach the dry goal.
  6. Rebuild. Roof, ceiling, drywall, flooring and paint are repaired once the structure is dry and the insurer approves the scope.

Storm damage vs. flood damage

The difference matters most for insurance. Water that comes in through a hole a storm made in the roof or a broken window is usually treated as storm or wind damage. Water that rises from the ground, such as an overflowing river, storm surge or flooded streets, is flood damage. FEMA notes that most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage and that flood policies through the National Flood Insurance Program typically have a 30-day waiting period. Many jobs after hurricanes involve both. See flood damage restoration.

Hidden storm damage to check for

  • Wet attic insulation and stained roof decking
  • Ceiling stains that appear days after the storm
  • Water inside walls below damaged windows
  • Damp carpet along exterior walls
  • Musty smells in the attic or basement

Hail damage in particular may not leak right away. If a storm passed through, have the roof looked at even if you see no water yet.

What affects the cost of storm damage restoration

  • Emergency work: the size of roof openings to tarp and windows to board up.
  • Water intrusion: how many rooms got wet and how long before drying started.
  • Debris removal from trees and structure.
  • Drying time and equipment.
  • Repairs to roofing, ceilings, walls and flooring.
  • Demand: after big regional storms, materials and crews are in short supply.

Insurance tips after a storm

  • Photograph and video all damage before cleanup, inside and out.
  • Make temporary repairs to prevent more damage, and keep every receipt.
  • Check your deductibles; many policies have a separate wind, hail or hurricane deductible.
  • Get written estimates and compare them with the adjuster's scope.
  • Do not sign over your insurance benefits without understanding the terms.

This is general information. Your policy and insurer decide what is covered.

How to prepare for storm season

  • Trim trees and branches that hang over the roof.
  • Clean gutters and downspouts before storm season.
  • Test your sump pump and consider a battery backup.
  • Know where your main water and electric shut-offs are.
  • Review your coverage, including whether you need flood insurance, well before a storm forms.
