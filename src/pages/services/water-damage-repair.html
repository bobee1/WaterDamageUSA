Quick answer: Water damage repair is the rebuild that follows drying: replacing wet drywall, insulation, flooring, baseboards, cabinets and paint. It should start only after moisture readings show the structure is dry, because closing up wet walls traps water and can lead to mold. The EPA notes that if you fix a mold problem but not the moisture, the mold comes back.

What water damage repair includes

  • Confirming the structure is dry with moisture readings
  • Replacing drywall and insulation removed during drying
  • Taping, mudding, texture matching and paint
  • Replacing or refinishing hardwood, laminate, vinyl and tile floors
  • New carpet pad and carpet, or reinstalling dried carpet
  • Baseboards, door casings and trim
  • Cabinet repair or replacement, including toe kicks and sink bases
  • Ceiling repair after leaks from above

The water damage repair process

  1. Dry verification. Moisture readings in the wet area match a dry reference area of the same material.
  2. Scope and estimate. The contractor lists every repair, matches materials and gives you a written estimate to compare with your insurer's scope.
  3. Prep and protection. Floors and furniture in the work area are covered, and dust control is set up.
  4. Rough repairs. Insulation, drywall, subfloor patches and any electrical or plumbing fixes by licensed trades.
  5. Finishes. Tape, mud and texture to match, primer and paint, then flooring, trim and cabinets.
  6. Final walk-through. You check the work against the scope before sign-off.

Why drying comes before repairs

It is tempting to patch a stain and paint over it. But water usually travels further than the stain shows, into wall cavities, under flooring and along framing. Sealing that moisture in with new drywall or flooring gives mold a dark, damp place to grow. The EPA's mold guidance is clear that moisture has to be controlled first. A proper water mitigation and structural drying job, finished and documented, protects the repair.

Repairs by room

  • Kitchens: dishwasher and sink leaks often ruin sink-base cabinets and the flooring under them. Cabinets may need to be pulled to dry the wall behind them.
  • Bathrooms: toilet, tub and shower leaks reach the subfloor and the ceiling below. Tile may need to come up to dry the subfloor.
  • Basements: finished walls usually need flood cuts, and carpet over concrete is hard to save after groundwater.
  • Ceilings: leaks from roofs, attics and upper floors cause stains and sagging. See ceiling water damage repair.

What affects the cost of water damage repair

  • Square footage of drywall, flooring and ceiling to replace.
  • Material matching. Discontinued flooring or custom cabinets may mean replacing more than the wet area.
  • Finish level. Texture matching, crown molding and specialty paint take more time.
  • Trades and permits. Electrical, plumbing or structural repairs add licensed trades and may need permits.
  • Mold found during repairs adds remediation before the rebuild can continue.

Always get a written estimate and compare it with the scope from your insurance adjuster.

DIY repair or hire a contractor?

A small drywall patch after a clean-water leak, once the area is confirmed dry, is a reasonable DIY job. Hire a contractor when the damage covers more than a few square feet, involves ceilings, flooring or cabinets, needs electrical or plumbing work, or is part of an insurance claim where the work must match the scope. Make sure any contractor holds the license your state and city require.

Insurance notes for repairs

When the water damage itself is covered, the policy usually pays to repair the resulting damage, minus your deductible. The cause of the leak, such as a worn-out water heater, may not be covered. Keep every estimate, invoice and photo, and get the adjuster's scope in writing before work starts. This is general information; your policy decides what is covered.
