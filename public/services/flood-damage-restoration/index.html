What flood cleanup includes

  • Safety check for electrical, gas and structural hazards before entry
  • Pumping out floodwater and removing mud and silt
  • Sorting contents: what can be cleaned and what must be discarded
  • Removing soaked carpet, pad, drywall and insulation
  • Cleaning and disinfecting floors, framing and hard surfaces
  • Structural drying with air movers and dehumidifiers
  • Mold prevention and, where needed, mold remediation
  • Photos, inventories and readings for flood insurance claims

Flood water is not like a pipe leak

Water from a broken supply line starts clean. Floodwater does not. It flows over streets, yards, septic systems and storm drains before it enters a home. EPA guidance warns that floodwater can carry sewage and other harmful substances, and that standing water and wet materials become a breeding ground for viruses, bacteria and mold. Under the ANSI/IICRC S500 standard, outside floodwater is treated as Category 3, the most contaminated level.

The flood damage restoration process

  1. Safe re-entry. Return only when local authorities allow it. The crew checks for downed lines, gas smells and weakened floors, and power to wet areas stays off.
  2. Water and mud removal. Submersible and trash pumps remove standing water; shovels and squeegees clear silt.
  3. Tear-out. Soaked drywall is cut out above the water line, and wet insulation, carpet and pad come out.
  4. Cleaning and disinfection. Framing, subfloors and hard surfaces are scrubbed and treated. EPA guidance warns never to mix cleaning products or add bleach to other chemicals.
  5. Drying. Air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture readings reach the dry goal. See structural drying.
  6. Rebuild. Repairs begin once the structure is dry and clean. See water damage repair.

EPA guidance sums it up: sort materials and throw away anything you cannot clean and dry. Photograph items before you discard them for your claim.

Health and safety during flood cleanup

  • Wear an N-95 respirator, goggles and gloves at a minimum, as EPA guidance advises.
  • Keep children, pregnant people and anyone with asthma or a weak immune system out of the cleanup.
  • Run generators and gas pumps only outside and at least 20 feet from the building.
  • Assume anything that sat in floodwater is contaminated until cleaned.
  • Wash hands often and keep cuts covered.

Flood insurance basics

FEMA notes that most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage, and that floods can happen anywhere. Flood coverage comes from a separate policy through the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP) or a private insurer, for homeowners, renters and businesses. New NFIP policies typically have a 30-day waiting period before coverage starts, so flood insurance has to be in place before a storm. If you have a policy, report the loss quickly, photograph everything and keep samples of flooring and materials if your adjuster asks for them.

What affects the cost of flood cleanup

  • Depth and spread of the water and how many rooms or floors it reached.
  • Mud and debris that must be removed and hauled away.
  • Tear-out of drywall, insulation and flooring.
  • Disinfection of all surfaces the water touched.
  • Drying time and the amount of equipment needed.
  • Mold if the water sat for days before cleanup began.

When to call a professional

Call for professional flood damage restoration when water reached living space, a finished basement or walls, when it sat for more than a day, or when you cannot safely clean while wearing protective gear. Professionals bring pumps, drying equipment and the moisture readings your claim may need.

Flood damage restoration questions

Is floodwater dangerous to clean up myself?

It can be. EPA flood cleanup guidance warns that floodwater can carry sewage and harmful substances, and advises that children, pregnant people and people with asthma or weakened immune systems should not take part in cleanup. Anyone cleaning should wear an N-95 respirator, goggles and gloves at a minimum.

What has to be thrown away after a flood?

Porous items that were soaked by floodwater, such as carpet and pad, upholstered furniture, mattresses, insulation and drywall, usually cannot be made safe and should be discarded. Hard, non-porous items can often be cleaned and disinfected.

Does homeowners insurance cover flood damage?

Usually not. FEMA notes that most homeowners insurance does not cover flood damage. Flood coverage comes from a separate policy, through the National Flood Insurance Program or a private insurer, and new NFIP policies typically have a 30-day waiting period.

How soon can flood cleanup start?

As soon as authorities say it is safe to return and the water has gone down. EPA guidance stresses drying the home and everything in it completely, and mold can begin to grow within 24 to 48 hours, so cleanup should start as soon as it is safe.

Can a flooded basement be saved?

Yes, in most cases. The structure can usually be cleaned, disinfected and dried. Finished walls, carpet and insulation that floodwater touched usually need to be removed and replaced.

Should I run a generator during cleanup?

Only outside, and at least 20 feet away from the building, as the EPA advises, to avoid carbon monoxide poisoning. Never run a generator or gas pump inside a home, basement or garage.
