What commercial water damage restoration includes

  • Fast assessment of the source, water category and affected areas
  • Containment to protect tenants, staff, customers and clean areas
  • Large-scale water extraction from carpet tile, concrete and hard floors
  • Moving or protecting furniture, inventory, files and equipment
  • Removing wet ceiling tiles, drywall and insulation
  • Drying with commercial air movers and dehumidifiers, sized to the space
  • Moisture logs and photo documentation for insurers and property managers
  • Repairs and coordination with building trades

The commercial restoration process

  1. Emergency response. The source is stopped, power to wet areas is made safe, and the water category is identified.
  2. Containment and access plan. Wet areas are sealed off, and equipment placement is planned around operations, exits and fire safety.
  3. Extraction. Standing water is removed quickly with commercial pumps and extractors. See water extraction.
  4. Tear-out and drying. Wet ceiling tiles, drywall and insulation are removed, and the space is dried to a documented goal. See structural drying.
  5. Cleaning. Areas touched by gray or black water are cleaned and disinfected.
  6. Repairs and handover. Ceilings, walls, flooring and finishes are restored, then the space is handed back to you.

Keeping the business running

  • Phased work: dry and return one area at a time instead of closing the whole site.
  • After-hours scheduling for tear-out and other noisy work.
  • Clear paths: hoses, cords and equipment kept out of exits and public areas.
  • Contents protection: moving or covering electronics, records and inventory.
  • Regular updates for owners, property managers and tenants.

Mold risk in commercial buildings

Large buildings have more places for water to hide. It sits above drop ceilings, inside walls, under carpet tile and in HVAC systems. For big mold problems, the EPA points schools and businesses to its remediation guide. It also says to fix the moisture source first. Fast drying, with readings to prove it, is the best way to avoid a mold job later. See mold remediation.

What affects the cost of commercial water damage restoration

  • Square footage and number of floors or units affected.
  • Water category: sprinkler and supply water versus sewage or floodwater.
  • Materials: carpet tile, drywall, ceiling tile, raised floors.
  • After-hours or phased work to keep operations going.
  • Contents and equipment that need moving, cleaning or drying.
  • Repairs and finishes to match existing build-outs.

Insurance and documentation

A building owner's policy, a landlord's policy and a tenant's policy may each pay for part of a loss. Business interruption coverage, if you have it, may help with lost income after a covered event. Flood needs its own policy, from FEMA's National Flood Insurance Program or a private insurer. Tell your insurer right away, and your landlord if you lease. Keep all moisture logs, photos and bills. This is general information, so review your policies with your agent.

Commercial water damage questions

Can my business stay open during water damage restoration?

Often, at least in part. Crews can seal off the wet area with plastic, keep hoses away from customer paths and do noisy work after hours. Whether you can stay open depends on safety, the size of the loss and the type of water.

What causes most commercial water damage?

Common causes include fire sprinkler discharges, burst or frozen pipes, roof leaks on flat roofs, restroom and kitchen plumbing failures, HVAC condensate leaks and flooding from storms. Multi-tenant buildings also see leaks that travel between units and floors.

Who is responsible for water damage in a leased space?

It depends on the lease and on where the water came from. Leases often split responsibility between the landlord, for the building and its systems, and the tenant, for its improvements and contents. Check the lease and notify both your landlord and your insurer.

Does business insurance cover lost income from water damage?

Business interruption coverage, where you have it, may pay for lost income and extra expenses while you cannot operate after a covered loss. Flood usually needs separate coverage. Review your commercial policy with your agent.

What documentation will my insurer want?

Photos and video of the damage, the cause, moisture readings and drying logs, an inventory of damaged contents and equipment, and invoices. The restoration crew's records cover most of this.
